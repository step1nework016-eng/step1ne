How to Get into Construction: A Complete Guide for Newcomers from Other Fields

By: Step1ne Executive Search Consultant Team Published 2026-08-31 About 6 min
Short answer

“Construction” isn't one job; it splits into at least four roles: BIM (modeling and coordination on the computer), engineering design (drafting and planning), site engineering (managing schedule and quality on site), and occupational safety (managing workplace safety and risk). People from other majors can get in, but what employers actually look at is whether you can read drawings and specifications, whether you're willing to learn, and whether you can communicate with people on site—not the name of your degree. Most roles can be entered through assistant or trainee positions, and you don't need a certification before applying.

What the construction industry does: four roles at a glance

The wave of semiconductor and high-tech plant construction in recent years has completely broken apart and reshuffled the work that falls under “construction”—not every role means baking in the sun and hauling rebar. Understanding which type might suit you matters more than rushing to send résumés.

Which one suits me?

If you're still unsure, here's a quick way to compare. No experience at all, but willing to learn software and fine with training first? BIM is a good entry point. Familiar with AutoCAD or Revit and able to read engineering drawings? Engineering design is a natural next step. Have site or construction coordination experience? A site engineer role puts it to direct use. Have an OSH background or certification? Occupational safety is your professional match. For a more visual comparison, see the "Which engineering role suits me?" cards in the Step1ne Construction Engineering column, which list the best-fit backgrounds for all four roles side by side.

Can you really get in without a related degree? What employers actually look for

Yes, but you need to apply for the right jobs. Semiconductor fab construction projects are breaking ground in quick succession, and demand is growing faster than the number of related graduates schools produce each year. That's where "any major, company-provided training" positions come from: companies have decided to train people themselves rather than wait for graduates in the field. When employers actually screen candidates, they usually look at the following.

  • Whether you can read drawings and follow the basic logic. You don't need to be able to draw, but with no spatial sense at all it will be tough.
  • Whether you're okay with training before you're up to speed. Most trainee roles include a period of training, and willingness to invest that time is a key screening point.
  • Whether you can communicate on site with people from different backgrounds. Construction projects involve owners, designers, and builders, so technical skill alone only goes so far.

Whether your degree is related usually affects your starting pay band, not whether you can apply — majors generally aren't a gate. You can confirm the specifics directly with the consultant or hiring manager during the interview.

Should you get certified before entering the industry?

It depends on the role—not every opening requires one. Most certifications can be earned on the job after you start, so they needn't hold you up before applying.

  • Occupational safety. You'll usually need the Class C Occupational Safety and Health Supervisor certificate — one of the few hard requirements for this role at the application stage.
  • Site engineering / site supervisor roles. By law, construction projects above a certain size must have qualified personnel such as a site director supervising on site, but entry-level openings usually don't require the applicant to hold that qualification already.
  • BIM. There's currently no legally required certification; vendor certifications such as Autodesk's are a plus, not an application requirement.
  • Engineering design. A technician certificate isn't required — practical ability, like whether you can draft and read codes and specifications, matters more than credentials.

Eligibility and exam schedules for certifications may change. A headhunter's view

Getting certified before job hunting is often the long way around. In our actual placements, many newcomers with no certificates at all landed training-track jobs because they were "willing to train first" and "good at reading drawings," and got certified after they started. Someone who spent six months on a certificate but never worked on a project often has a weaker résumé than someone with "six months of hands-on experience." Employers want to know whether you can do the work, and a piece of paper can't prove that.

No experience at all: how to negotiate your first job

The easiest ways into the construction industry right now are trainee and assistant roles. When a company says outright "no experience needed, training provided," it means they plan to train the people they need rather than wait for ready-made candidates to appear. These roles usually share a few traits.

  • There is a set training period; you may be trained elsewhere before being stationed at the project site.
  • On site, senior staff or a PM will help you get up to speed.
  • Starting pay is usually set based on education and how relevant your background is; being "inexperienced" won't get you turned down for an interview.

In the interview, we suggest asking directly: how long the training period for this role is, how pay is calculated during training, and what the actual split of daily work is once you're up to speed (how much modeling, meetings, and site visits). Getting clear answers to these is far more accurate than generic information you find online.

What new hires face in their first three months

Whichever role you take, the most important thing in the first three months is building basic judgment: learning the project's drawing system, understanding the company's naming conventions and procedures, and knowing whom to check with when problems come up. Making mistakes during this period is normal; what causes trouble is "pretending to understand and changing things on your own." Asking questions directly is the most cost-effective way to learn early on in this industry.

FAQ

Can I really apply with no engineering background at all?+

Yes, especially for openings that state "no experience required, training provided." Employers want people who are willing to learn and can follow basic logic; an engineering background is not a hard requirement. Be honest about your background in the interview and let the consultant or hiring manager judge the fit — don't assume up front that you won't be considered.

Do I have to get certified before I can apply?+

Not necessarily. Roles like occupational safety usually require a relevant technician certification, but most roles (BIM, engineering design, entry-level site jobs) let you get in first and earn certifications while employed. Spending time on certifications without hands-on experience won't necessarily put your résumé ahead of “people already doing the work.”

Of the four roles, which is the most beginner-friendly?+

Looking at the training-track openings on the market now, BIM is usually the lowest-barrier way in: most of the time is spent in the office, the company provides software training, and it's less physically demanding than site work. The other roles are open too, but the entry path differs: one starts with training, the other requires relevant experience first.

What should I prepare before the interview?+

First work out which kind of role you want to apply for, then prepare accordingly: think through why you want to enter this industry, whether you can accept a training period and possible on-site assignments, and where your bottom line is on pay and working style. The advantage of applying through a headhunter is that you can confirm the actual details of the role with a consultant first, instead of finding out at the interview that the terms don't match your expectations.
