How to Find a Headhunting Consultant: How They Source Talent and How to Work with Them

By the Step1ne executive search consultant team Published 2026-10-19 About 8 min read
Short answer

A headhunting consultant is a recruitment professional who proactively finds people on a company's behalf. Unlike job boards that wait for applications, they reach out directly to people who aren't job hunting yet. To find one, companies can start with three channels—referrals from peers, LinkedIn and search engines—then filter on three things: does the firm hold an employment services license, does it know your industry, and can it explain how it will find people. Before working together, put when fees are charged, how long the guarantee period is, and how candidate ownership is determined in the contract.

1What is a headhunting consultant? How they differ from HR and job boards

A headhunter is a recruiter commissioned by a company to find people for specific positions, usually working for a licensed executive search or HR consulting firm. The job isn't posting ads and waiting for resumes; it's first understanding exactly whom the company needs, then going out into the market to find people who fit, and staying with the process until they start.

Many companies, on first encountering headhunters, confuse them with job sites or their own HR team's work. Here's the difference in one table:

In-house hiring by the company's HRJob board postingsHeadhunting consultant
How people are foundPostings and referrals; wait for applicationsVisibility on a platform, waiting for applicationsActive searching and direct outreach
Who you can reachPeople currently looking for workPeople currently looking for workIncludes people who aren't job hunting but are a good fit
Who does the screeningHRHRConsultants screen first; you only review the shortlist
How you payStaff time costPosting fee, depending on the platform's planMostly charged after the candidate starts
Suitable rolesGeneral openings, and openings that are attractive enough on their ownGeneral roles, high-volume rolesHard-to-fill, scarce, confidential or highly contested roles

The three approaches don't replace one another. Most companies fill ordinary roles themselves and hand only hard-to-fill or confidential roles to headhunting consultants. For a full comparison of hiring channels, see How companies find talent.

2When should a company bring in a headhunting consultant?

Not every role needs a headhunter. When you see the following, it's usually time to bring in a headhunting consultant:

  • The opening has been up for ages, and applicants aren't the right fit: The right people are mostly already employed and doing well — they won't go to job sites and apply on their own.
  • Unclear on market rates: You're not sure what salary this position needs right now to attract people. Headhunting consultants talk to candidates every day, so they have a better feel for the market.
  • The role can't be advertised publicly: for example, replacing a current manager, preparing a new business, or not wanting competitors to see your plans—public postings can actually be risky for these hires.
  • All the résumés look great and you can't tell who can really do the job: the consultant talks in depth with candidates first, does preliminary checks, and notes risk points in the recommendation report.
  • HR is short-handed: screening résumés, scheduling interviews, and chasing progress take a lot of time. Hand that off, and HR only needs to interview people who've already been screened.
  • What key talent cares about can't be written into a job description: team culture, manager style, and future growth need a consultant to explain one-on-one before candidates are willing to talk.

3How do headhunting consultants find people? The full process

This is what companies ask about most, and what's least often explained clearly. An experienced headhunting consultant, after taking on a role, generally works like this:

Step one: get clear on who you're actually looking for

The consultant confirms with the hiring manager what problem this role actually needs to solve, rather than just searching for keywords from the job description. For example, two "sales manager" roles—one opening new accounts, one maintaining key accounts—need completely different people. If this step isn't done well, no amount of hard searching afterwards will fix the direction.

Step 2: Draw a talent map

The consultant maps out which companies and positions this kind of talent is likely in right now, including direct competitors, upstream and downstream companies, and companies that have done projects of a similar scale. This map determines where to look.

Step 3: Reach out proactively through multiple channels

Common channels include:

  • Our own talent database: records of candidates we've contacted and spoken with before, usually the fastest source.
  • Professional networks such as LinkedIn: search by job title, company and skills, then reach out by direct message.
  • Referrals: ask people in the industry for recommendations, or ask suitable people who aren't looking to move to introduce others they know.
  • Public information: industry news, conference speakers, and published project results often surface people who aren't active on social media.
  • Job postings: a supporting channel to attract people who are actively job hunting.

Step 4: Talk in depth with candidates and do a first round of screening

Once we reach candidates who are interested, the consultant first talks with them about what they've done, why they're considering a move, their expected salary and terms, and when they could start. Anyone whose terms clearly don't match is screened out at this stage and never passed to the company.

Step 5: Write a recommendation report, don't just forward résumés

A good referral includes not just the résumé but also why the person fits and what to watch out for, such as higher-than-usual salary expectations or the reason one stint was short. The company can decide whether to interview just by reading the report.

Step 6: arrange interviews and support salary negotiation through to the start date

The consultant coordinates interview schedules, checks in with each side separately after interviews, helps bridge gaps during salary negotiation, and keeps confirming the candidate hasn't changed their mind until the start date. Some consultants also help run reference checks with former managers, with the company's consent.

Put simply, what matters in how a headhunter finds people isn't how big their database is, but whether they can pin down the need, know where the people are and persuade them to talk. When choosing a consultant, ask them to explain how they'd search for your role—that's where the difference shows.

4I want a headhunting consultant: where to find one and how to judge

Three common ways to search

  • Referrals from peers or friends: ask HR staff or managers in the same industry and function who they've used. It's the fastest way to learn "what it's actually like to work with them."
  • LinkedIn search: search "headhunting consultant" plus your industry or function, then look at what they've shared and the types of roles they've handled to judge whether they know your field.
  • Search engines: Search "industry name + headhunter," find firms that publish content about that industry, and ask them to assign a consultant who knows it.

Three questions to judge whether a consultant is good

  1. Do they hold a legal employment service license? In Taiwan, private employment service businesses must obtain a license from the authorities. You can ask them for the license number and verify it.
  2. Do they know your industry and function? Ask them to describe the current market for this kind of role and where the people usually are—only those who can answer with specifics really know it.
  3. Can they explain how they'd find someone like you? "We have a big database" isn't enough; a good consultant will explain which companies they'd look at and how they'd reach out.

5Working with a headhunting consultant: the process and terms to agree up front

Working with a headhunting consultant generally goes through these stages:

  1. Needs briefing: you explain the role, team situation, salary range, and desired start date.
  2. Sign an engagement contract: spell out the fee structure, guarantee period, and candidate ownership.
  3. Consultant search and recommendations: once you receive a recommendation report, reply quickly on whether you'll interview. The faster you reply, the less likely the candidate is to be snapped up elsewhere.
  4. Interviews and salary negotiation: after each interview, share your feedback with the consultant so they can adjust the search.
  5. Candidate start and guarantee period: after the start date the guarantee period begins; departures within it are handled according to the contract.

The issues that most often cause problems are the following terms not being spelled out before working together:

TermsWhat to ask clearly
When you're chargedDo they charge after the candidate starts, or collect part upfront at signing?
How fees are calculatedA number of months' salary, or a percentage of annual salary? Is salary counted as base pay only, or including bonuses?
Guarantee periodHow long is it? If the candidate leaves within the guarantee period, is it a free replacement or a refund?
Candidate ownershipFor candidates already recommended, how long does a hire still count as that consultant's placement?
Exclusive or non-exclusiveCan I engage other firms for the same role at the same time?
Key regulations
  • Placement fees may only be collected after the employment contract takes effect (Taiwan's Regulations on the Permission and Management of Private Employment Services Agencies, Article 4, Paragraph 2)
  • If employment ends within 40 days after the employment contract takes effect for reasons attributable to the job seeker, the employer may request one free re-referral or a refund of 50% of the placement fee (Article 4, Paragraph 3 of the same Regulations)
  • Registration and placement fees charged to employers are capped: if the employee's first-month salary is at or below the average wage, the total may not exceed the first month's salary; if above, it may not exceed four months' salary (Taiwan's Standards for Fee Items and Amounts Charged by Private Employment Service Agencies, Article 3)

6What happens when two headhunters submit the same candidate?

It's common, and reasonable, for a company to engage two or three headhunters at once — it's like opening more pools to search. But the most common dispute in that situation is: the same candidate is referred by both firms — after the hire, whose fee is it?

The way to avoid disputes is to set the rules in the contract upfront. A common approach is that the firm that referred the candidate first, with a written record (such as the time the referral report was sent), takes precedence. When a company receives a duplicate candidate, it should also notify the later firm right away rather than waiting until after an offer is made.

Conversely, if you're a job seeker, don't let different headhunters submit you to the same company; the employer will see your job search as disorganized, and it will count against you.

7When you don't actually need a headhunting consultant

  • The job itself is attractive enough: if pay, location, and brand are competitive, the right people will apply once you post it, with no need to pay extra.
  • High-volume, high-turnover frontline roles: for retail, customer service, or warehouse roles, job banks, recruitment agencies, or staffing are usually more cost-effective. See How to choose outsourced recruiting for entry- and mid-level roles.
  • Short-term staff, or when you're not sure you need them long-term: temporary staffing is a better fit. For the cost structure, see How are staffing costs calculated?.
  • Requirements aren't clear yet: if the job content, salary range, and interviewers haven't been decided, bringing in a headhunter just means changing direction back and forth. We recommend aligning internally first, then engaging a firm.

8FAQ

What's the difference between a headhunting consultant and a headhunting firm?+

A headhunting firm is a company licensed to provide employment services; a headhunting consultant is the person at that firm who actually does the searching. Companies sign with the firm, but the person you deal with every day and who finds people for you is the consultant. So besides the firm, look at whether the consultant assigned to you knows your industry.

How do headhunters find people who aren't looking for a job?+

Mainly through talent mapping plus direct outreach: first list the companies where such talent is likely to be, then contact people directly via professional networks like LinkedIn, referrals, and public industry information, explain the opportunity, and see whether they're open to a conversation.

Do I have to pay a headhunting consultant up front?+

Most headhunters charge on success, only when the candidate starts. Under Taiwanese regulations, placement fees can only be collected after the employment contract takes effect; if a firm asks for a payment when you sign, ask exactly what it is for and whether it will be refunded if no one is found.

Can you work with several headhunting consultants at once?+

Yes, unless you've signed an exclusive agreement. When working with several firms at once, set the ownership rule for duplicate referrals in the contract, and notify the later firm right away when you receive a duplicate candidate.

How soon can a headhunter recommend candidates?+

It depends on the role's difficulty, pay competitiveness, and how much talent is on the market; there's no standard timeline. During the needs interview, you can ask the consultant to explain the expected timeline and when the first recommendations will come, and agree on how often they'll report progress.
