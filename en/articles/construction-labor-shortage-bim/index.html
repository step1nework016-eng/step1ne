Is construction really short of workers? Why BIM engineers are in demand anyway

By the Step1ne Executive Search Consultant Team Published 2026-09-03 About 7 min read
Short answer

The shortage is real, but what's missing is mainly frontline site labor. Statistics from Taiwan's National Land Management Agency (Ministry of the Interior) put the nationwide construction labor shortage at over 46,000 people, with frontline worker vacancies making up more than 70% (Commercial Times report, August 3, 2026). Office-based technical and coordination roles like BIM engineers aren't counted in that figure, yet in our work with hiring companies, competition for these roles is no less intense than for site labor, because the two have completely different supply-and-demand structures.

The official numbers: how many workers is construction really short?

Let's get the numbers straight first, rather than repeating figures that circulate in the industry by impression. According to the "Construction Industry Economic Overview" published by the National Land Management Agency under Taiwan's Ministry of the Interior, as reported by the Commercial Times on August 3, 2026:

  • Taiwan's construction industry is short an estimated more than 46,000 workers
  • Of these, frontline worker vacancies number 32,525, more than 70% of the total shortage and its main source
  • Broken down, unfilled general-labor positions number about 9,917, and unfilled skilled-trade positions about 22,000
  • Regional breakdown: central Taiwan has the most at 10,248, followed by northern Taiwan at 9,977, then southern Taiwan at 8,082
  • Migrant worker employment: 92.7% of construction companies do not employ migrant workers. The share rises with company size; among companies with 300 or more employees, 81.6% employ migrant workers — the highest of any size bracket

Source: the "Construction Industry Economic Overview" survey by Taiwan's National Land Management Agency, Ministry of the Interior, as cited in a Commercial Times report on August 3, 2026. The official release remains authoritative for methodology and sample; this article only summarizes it and makes no projections of its own.

Why the shortage got this bad: what the Legislative Yuan's research report says

The labor shortage didn't happen overnight. A research report published in August 2023 by the research and evaluation unit of Taiwan's Legislative Yuan found that the job vacancy rate in construction rose from 2.26% in 2018 (ROC year 107) to 5.14% in 2021 (ROC year 110). The causes it cited include a shrinking workforce due to low birth rates, young people being put off by the industry's high occupational injury rate, and a wave of simultaneous demand — Taiwanese companies reshoring and building plants, the Forward-Looking Infrastructure Program, urban renewal and rebuilding of aging structures — all concentrating labor demand into the same period.

Source: Legislative Yuan (Taiwan), "Analysis of Labor Shortages in the Construction Industry", written August 2023. This provides background on structural causes and is not the latest shortage data; for the actual gap, refer to the 2026 figures in the previous section.

Why BIM engineers aren't part of this shortage, yet are just as much in demand

Seeing the statistics above, it's easy to assume "the whole construction industry is short of people, so any job is easy to get." But the gap is clearly defined: it counts frontline site labor (general laborers and skilled tradespeople), not the technical and management roles in the office that do planning, modeling and coordination. BIM engineers belong to the latter and don't appear in the "frontline labor vacancies" statistic.

Yet among the employers we actually work with, competition for BIM talent is no less intense than for on-site staff, and some positions even offer "any major, company-provided training" (see Can you switch to a BIM engineer career without a related degree?). That is usually a direct sign of a supply-demand imbalance, not corporate generosity. There are two reasons:

  • The fab construction boom in semiconductors and AI-related industries is driving two kinds of talent demand at once. The tech plant projects breaking ground in quick succession in recent years need both on-site workers to get the buildings built and BIM teams to integrate the MEP, structural, and architectural models across disciplines. Both needs have surged at the same time, but the number of BIM-related graduates schools produce each year can't keep up
  • Some developers treat BIM as a way to reduce their reliance on site labor, and in doing so they have actually driven up demand for BIM staff. According to public reports, Highwealth Construction (興富發建設) invested about NT$500 million in 2022 and spent ten years building a BIM team of more than 100 people, running the construction process on computer first, with the goal of cutting labor needs by 20–30%. This logic is not unusual in the industry: to use technology to depend less on frontline labor, you first need enough BIM staff to build that process. In effect, part of the pressure of "finding workers" has turned into the pressure of "finding BIM engineers"

Source for the 興富發 case: CTWANT, report dated April 5, 2022. This is a single company's public case, used to illustrate industry thinking. It is not industry-wide data, and it dates from several years ago, so please treat it as reference only.

A headhunter's view

Among the construction and building-related openings we see, on-site entry-level roles and BIM/technical roles are two completely different levels of difficulty. On-site entry-level roles usually stall because of a willingness problem: the pay and working conditions aren't attractive enough to the local workforce. BIM roles usually stall because of a supply problem: people with the required profile (Revit skills, cross-discipline coordination, willingness to work on site) are simply scarce in the market. The two bottlenecks call for completely different solutions, and lumping them together as a “labor shortage” easily leads to the wrong diagnosis.

These two things don't contradict each other; they are two completely different kinds of labor shortage within the same industry. The overall labor shortage in construction is real, but if you conclude that "all construction-related jobs are hard to fill," you'll miss a different supply-demand imbalance happening in technology-transition roles like BIM.

If you're considering this direction, what this means for you

An imbalance between supply and demand is a neutral fact for job seekers, not a guarantee. It means companies are currently more willing to invest in training and relax degree requirements to fill these roles, so the barrier to entry is relatively friendly. It does not mean pay will keep rising, or that the job suits everyone; adaptability on site, willingness to learn and the ability to communicate across disciplines are still what actually get people hired. FAQ

Does the construction labor shortage mean BIM engineers are scarce too?+

They are separate statistics, but they point the same way. Official labor-shortage figures count frontline workers on site (general laborers and skilled trades) and don't include technical roles like BIM. But from a hands-on recruiting perspective, BIM openings are just as hard to fill — only for a different reason: it's not a willingness problem, it's that talent development can't keep pace with demand from the fab construction boom.

Why do BIM job ads often say "any major, training provided"?+

Because companies have done the math: waiting for "related degree + experience" candidates won't get them the headcount they need from the market. Training people themselves beats waiting, and large fab construction projects that need many people at once almost always post roles like this.

Will the labor shortage keep pushing BIM engineer salaries up?+

The supply-demand imbalance does support salaries, but that doesn't mean they'll rise without limit, and pay depends heavily on level. For actual salary bands, see BIM engineer salary ranges. We can't guarantee where salaries will go; all we can say is that what companies are offering now reflects the current balance of supply and demand.

Is now a good time to move into construction or BIM?+

From a supply-and-demand standpoint, companies are indeed more willing right now to invest in training and relax degree requirements, so the barrier to entry is relatively friendly. But whether it's a “good time” also depends on whether it suits you: adaptability on site, willingness to learn, and cross-disciplinary communication remain the keys to lasting. This article doesn't promise it's “definitely worth it”; it only offers a basis for judging the current situation.
