Engineering Design Engineer vs. Site Engineer: two different career paths, which should you choose?

By the Step1ne Executive Search Consultant Team Published 2026-09-30
Short answer

Engineering design engineers and site (project) engineers are both called engineers and their pay ranges overlap (both NT$35,000–45,000 during training and NT$50,000–60,000 at senior level), but the actual work is different. Design engineers mainly produce drawings in AutoCAD and Revit—their focus is the drawings. Site (project) engineers mainly track construction progress, run quality inspections and coordinate with subcontractors—their focus is people and the site. Both go to the construction site (neither is a pure desk job); the difference is that design engineers go to check the drawings against reality, while site engineers go to manage progress. Which path to choose depends on whether you'd rather get the details exactly right on paper or work through problems with people on site.

Engineering design engineers and site engineers sound alike—do they actually do the same work?

No. In high-tech fab construction roles, for example, the two positions sit in different departments, and their responsibilities are designed to be separate.

Engineering design engineers sit in the "design department." Their core work is CAD drafting (AutoCAD, Revit) for the various system works in high-tech plants; they also help with site surveys to make sure drawings match conditions on site, and complete tasks assigned by their manager in line with project schedules. Put simply, the output is drawings; site visits are about getting the drawings right, not managing the site.

Site (project) engineers belong to the "engineering department." Their core work is on-site schedule control, quality inspection, coordinating interfaces between trades, and managing subcontractors. They also oversee the planning, design, and construction of fab process piping and MEP systems, and prepare and keep construction records. Their output is "the site gets built on time and to spec"; drawings are a tool for checking against the site, not the main deliverable.

Both say "100% on site," but isn't an engineering design engineer supposed to be drawing in an office?

This is the most commonly misunderstood point. People hear "drawing" and assume office work, but design engineers on high-tech fab projects actually work like site engineers: they're stationed at the project's site office, and the work environment is likewise listed as "100% on site."

The difference isn't "whether you go on site" but "what you do there." Design engineers go on site to check that drawings match actual construction (site surveys), and spend most of their time drawing on a computer in the site office. Site engineers, by contrast, are directly involved in schedule control, quality inspection, and coordinating with subcontractors — the site itself is their workplace. Drawings are a tool to support their judgment, not something they produce.

If you chose engineering design thinking it means "an office job with no site visits," you'll find a gap once you start. Be sure to confirm this beforehand.

Pay and education requirements: how do the two paths differ?

The salary ranges actually overlap—it's not that “site pays more” or “design pays more”:

  • Trainee engineering design engineer: monthly salary NT$35,000 to NT$45,000. No relevant experience required, but you need basic AutoCAD or Revit drafting skills, the ability to read civil/architectural/MEP/plumbing and electrical drawings, and a strong willingness to learn.
  • Senior engineering design engineer: NT$50,000–60,000 per month; requires advanced AutoCAD and Revit drafting skills plus experience in plant or engineering design.
  • Trainee semiconductor project engineer (on-site): NT$35,000–45,000 a month. No related experience required, but you need basic office software skills, communication and coordination skills, and a strong willingness to learn.
  • Senior Semiconductor Project Engineer (site side): NT$50,000–60,000 per month; requires prior experience supervising construction of semiconductor fabs or plant office buildings, or in electrical and mechanical work.

Both require at least a junior college degree, but the eligible majors differ. Design engineers lean toward industrial design, civil engineering (including construction), and architecture (or interior design). Site engineers have a broader range that also includes electrical engineering and mechanical engineering (including HVAC/refrigeration and energy), because the electromechanical systems they plan and supervise on site cover more ground.

Whichever path you apply for, what skills or certifications do you need?

The core requirement for engineering design engineers is software skill: proficiency in AutoCAD and Revit drafting directly determines whether you can get up to speed, and senior roles explicitly require "advanced" drafting ability; this is the toughest technical bar on this path. International AutoCAD or Revit certifications and experience with tendering, shop drawings, or as-built drawings are pluses.

For site (project) engineers, the core requirements are communication and coordination skills plus on-site judgment. The job requirements list CAD software not as mandatory but as a plus: certifications such as Public Works Quality Management Personnel or Class A Occupational Safety and Health Supervisor for the construction industry, or basic AutoCAD drafting/drawing-reading skills, can earn a better salary offer but aren't required to apply. The real must-haves are being able to accept assignment to the company's existing project sites and being able to communicate and coordinate with internal teams and subcontractors.

Both paths accept people without related experience who come in through training; the difference is what you're trained in. On the design side it's software skills and reading drawings; on the site side it's hands-on experience in site coordination and construction management.

Long-term career: where do these two paths lead?

The usual division of labor in the industry is this: the design side turns the architect's or owner's drawings into detailed construction drawings, and with experience moves deeper into drawing precision, BIM integration, and design coordination. The site side is closer to a supervision/project management role, overseeing whether the contractor is building to the drawings and controlling schedule and quality; with experience, people tend to move toward site manager or project manager roles (Source: [一次盤點工程師6大種類](https://www.hnl-consultants.com/%E5%B7%A5%E7%A8%8B%E5%B8%AB/), [建築工程監造全解析](https://twprobe.com/jianzhu-gongcheng-jianzao-zhineng-zongjian-zhinan/)).

Note that "construction supervision" has a specific legal definition (an independent third party appointed by the owner to check whether the contractor is building to the drawings), and it is not quite the same legal role as job titles like "engineering design engineer" or "site (project) engineer" inside a private construction company. What we are describing here is how the actual work is divided, not the legal qualification for construction supervision. If this distinction matters to you, we recommend asking the hiring department directly how the role is actually positioned before you apply.

Not sure which path suits you? How to decide

Some questions to ask yourself:

  • Are you better at focusing on a screen for long stretches, drawing to millimeter precision? Or do you get restless sitting still and prefer going on site to see conditions firsthand and coordinate with different people?
  • Are you interested in, or do you have a foundation in, drafting software like AutoCAD or Revit? If you've never touched it and have no interest in learning, the site side is a more realistic starting point.
  • Can you handle long hours in a site environment (sun, noise, dealing with subcontractors), or do you prefer fixed work in the site office? The design side carries relatively less site management responsibility.
  • Both paths require accepting assignments to sites in other cities and counties; there's no difference here, so it isn't a factor in choosing.

Related openings: Senior Engineering Design Engineer, Trainee Engineering Design Engineer (design side); Senior Semiconductor Project Engineer, Trainee Semiconductor Project Engineer (site side), covering Taoyuan–Hsinchu–Miaoli, Taichung, and Yunlin in Taiwan.

## FAQ **Q1. Do engineering design engineers never have to go on site?** A1. No. The work environment for engineering design engineers is also listed as "100% on site." They are stationed at the project's site office and help with site surveys and with checking that drawings match actual construction. Their main output is still drawings, not site management. **Q2. Do site (project) engineers have to know AutoCAD?** A2. Not necessarily. In the job requirements, AutoCAD drafting or drawing-reading skills are a plus, not a requirement. The real requirements are communication and coordination skills, willingness to be assigned to project locations, and basic office skills. Knowing the software can earn you a better salary offer, but it is not a condition for applying. **Q3. Which path pays more?** A3. The salary ranges overlap, so neither is simply higher. The trainee track on both sides pays NT$35,000–45,000 per month, and the experienced track on both sides pays NT$50,000–60,000 per month. Actual pay depends on your background, skills and interview performance. The difference lies in the job content, not the starting pay structure. **Q4. Can I apply to either path without a related degree?** A4. Neither trainee track requires related work experience, but there are still degree requirements. The design side focuses on industrial design, civil engineering (including construction) and architecture (or interior design). The site side is broader and also includes electrical engineering and mechanical engineering (including refrigeration/HVAC and energy). As long as your major is within range, you can apply for the trainee positions without hands-on experience. **Q5. Can I switch between the design side and the site side later?** A5. The positions themselves don't restrict switching, but in practice the two sides build different skills. The design side builds drafting and drawing-integration skills, while the site side builds hands-on site management and coordination. Changing direction usually means filling in the core skills the other path requires (for example, moving from design to site means gaining site management experience; moving from site to design means building advanced drafting skills). **Q6. Is "construction supervision" (監造) the same as the site (project) engineer described here?** A6. Not exactly. Under Taiwanese regulations, "construction supervision" is an independent third-party role appointed by the project owner to check that the contractor builds according to the drawings. The site (project) engineer discussed here is an internal job title at a private engineering company, focused on site schedule control, quality inspection and subcontractor coordination. The duties are similar, but the legal status is different.
