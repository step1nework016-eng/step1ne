What customer support work is really like: how the four types differ

By: Step1ne Executive Search Consultants Published 2026-07-27 About 5 min
Short answer

"Customer service" is a very broad label, and the actual jobs differ a lot. Game support is mostly text-based, handling account issues and player disputes; payment support is almost entirely text, and because money is involved, there is little tolerance for errors; brand support is mainly inbound and outbound phone calls; technical support requires understanding product details. The one question you should ask before applying, but almost nobody does, is: "What is the split between phone and text?" That question decides whether you will last in the job more than the salary does.

A headhunter's view

Before applying for customer service, the question you most need to ask but almost nobody does is: "Roughly what share of this role is phone versus text?"

The answer to this question matters more than salary in whether you'll last. Some people are fine on the phone but type slowly; others write well but tense up the moment they pick up a call. If either type ends up in the wrong kind of customer service role, they won't make it past three months. And job descriptions often just say "phone, email, live chat," without showing the real mix.

What does a day actually look like?

Taking a role centered on text-based support as an example, a typical day looks roughly like this:

  • Before going live: check handover notes, the day's announcements and promotion changes (this part is crucial; miss it and you'll give wrong answers)
  • Most of the working hours: handling multiple chat windows at once and replying per SOP; transferring or escalating issues beyond your authority
  • Tasks in between: monitoring systems or social media, replying to comments and DMs, supporting campaigns during promotional periods
  • Before clocking out: close cases, log unresolved ones and hand them over to the next shift

Phone support has a different rhythm: calls come to you, and when it's busy they come one after another with no breather, but each case usually wraps up faster.

What do people who stay long-term have in common?

From our actual placement experience, the people who stick with customer service and move up aren't necessarily "the most even-tempered" — they're the ones with these three qualities:

  1. Separating emotions from the issue: the customer is angry at the situation, not at you. People who last aren't unaffected; they can switch off once they hang up
  2. Patience with process—customer service comes with lots of SOPs and record-keeping requirements. People who think “this is a hassle, I'll just handle it myself” usually don't last, because incomplete records eventually come back to bite you
  3. Asking instead of bluffing: escalating or checking with a supervisor when you're unsure is far safer than guessing an answer for the customer
A headhunter's view

In customer service interviews, what hiring managers most often screen out isn't "lack of experience" but answers that don't show steadiness, such as talking about a previous job and doing nothing but complain about customers or managers.

Customer service is heavy emotional labor, so managers want people who "stay steady when things go badly." When asked in an interview how you'd handle a tough complaint, the point isn't how good you are at soothing people but whether you can explain your decision logic in the moment: what you handle yourself, what you escalate, and how you document it afterward.

Can I do it without experience?

Yes. Customer service is one of the few job types that's genuinely friendly to people without experience; most contact centers have full training and mentoring programs. What entry-level roles actually require is:

  • Chinese typing speed (text-based support usually requires 50+ characters per minute)
  • Clear spoken communication (tested in practice for phone-based roles)
  • Able to work shifts and follow company policies

Related industry experience is a plus, not a requirement. For example, in game support, people who've actually played that type of game get up to speed noticeably faster because they understand what players are talking about.

Five things to confirm before you apply

  1. What's the actual split between phone and text?
  2. What's the current turnover like on this shift?
  3. Is there a KPI? How is it calculated, and what happens if you miss it?
  4. How long is the training, and how is pay calculated during it?
  5. What's the escalation process for complaints beyond your authority?

Question 5 is especially important. On a team with a clear escalation process, the pressure on the front line is much lower; if instead you have to carry everything yourself, the job will be very hard.

FAQ

Do customer service reps get yelled at a lot?+

You will deal with emotional customers; that's part of the job. But the intensity varies more than most people think. Text-based roles (such as payments and some game support) are noticeably less stressful than inbound phone lines, and in teams with complete SOPs and escalation procedures, the front line has much less to shoulder alone. When applying, be sure to ask about the mix of communication channels and how escalation works.

Do you need to be good at games to work in game support?+

It isn't required, but it's a clear advantage. People who have actually played that type of game understand the situations players describe, judge much faster, and are less likely to misunderstand what's needed. Most job listings treat "enjoys playing games in this genre" as a plus rather than a requirement.

Is there a career path in customer service?+

There are three common paths: go deeper into a specialty (moving into higher-barrier areas like finance or technical support), move into management (shift lead, team lead, customer service manager), or switch sideways (sales, product operations, customer success). The key is whether your first job puts you on a team with clear levels and a real training program.

Should I take a contract customer service job?+

Look at what happens when the contract ends. Six-month or one-year contracts aren't necessarily worse; many are project-based or cover someone on parental leave. The key is to ask upfront: is there an evaluation for renewal or conversion to permanent when the term ends, and roughly what share of people have actually been converted in the past?

Is typing speed really tested?+

Text-based customer service roles usually do test this — a common requirement is 50 characters per minute or more. It's something you can build quickly: practicing with an online typing test for a few days before the interview is usually enough. Phone-based roles more often test spoken communication and how you handle callers instead.
