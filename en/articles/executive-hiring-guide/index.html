A senior opening has been up for six months and still no hire?

By Step1ne Executive Search Team Published 2026-07-27 About 9 min
Short answer

When senior roles stay unfilled for a long time, more than 80% of the time it isn't that "there's no one in the market"; the process itself is driving people away. The five most common sticking points: the job description lists requirements instead of the problems to solve, the salary band lags the market, decision-makers join the process too late, the interview design isn't adjusted to the level of the role, and confidentiality and internal communication aren't planned. All five sit on the company's side, which means all five can be fixed.

First, drop one assumption: are there really no people out there?

When a senior role has been open for six months, the most common conclusion inside the company is “there just aren't enough people like this on the market.” Sometimes that's true: in some extremely niche areas (say, a project lead who understands both a specific manufacturing process and overseas plant construction), there may be only twenty-some people in all of Taiwan.

But more often, that's not the case. When we take on these "searching forever" assignments, our first step is usually a market mapping: listing every person who fits the criteria one by one. The result is often a list of thirty or forty people, a dozen or so of whom could be approached on reasonable terms. The people exist; the problem is why they don't come in, or why they leave after joining.

So before raising the budget or loosening requirements, it's worth checking these five things.

Sticking point 1: the job description is written as “requirements,” not as “problems to solve”

This is the most common one. Most senior job descriptions look like this: ten-plus years of experience, managed a team of thirty or more, familiar with ERP systems, able to communicate across borders, bachelor's degree or above…

These are screening criteria, not what the job actually is. The problem is that the people who could do this job mostly have good jobs already. They aren't short of positions that match their profile. What they lack is a reason worth moving for.

What really gets senior candidates' attention is spelling out three things:

  • What problem does this position exist to solve: taking over a department that already runs well, or rebuilding an organization in disarray? These two attract completely different people
  • What success looks like — what would this person need to have achieved a year from now for the company to feel it hired the right person?
  • What resources and constraints there are—what the budget is, whether you can build your own team, how many layers above you need convincing. Being honest about constraints won't scare people off; being vague will
A headhunter's view

When we take on a senior search, there's one question we usually ask at the first meeting with the hiring team: "If someone were doing an excellent job in this role today, what would be different about the company a year from now?"

Searches where the client can answer this usually produce results in two to three months. Those where they can't, answering "just manage the department well" or "make operations run a bit smoother," tend to drag on, because even internally there's no agreement on what this person should do.

When a role stays open for a long time, sometimes the real problem isn't that you can't find anyone; it's that you haven't yet worked out what you're hiring them to do. It's not pleasant to hear, but it's the most common root cause we've seen.

Sticking point 2: the salary band lags the market, and nobody has noticed

Salary bands are often set three to five years ago, and the market has moved a long way since. Without an internal benchmark, companies find it hard to notice their numbers are no longer competitive.

A public benchmark you can measure against: according to the Taiwan Ministry of Labor's "Occupational Wage Survey for ROC year 114" (published May 2026, valid sample of 9,977 businesses), average annual earnings in ROC year 113 were highest for managers and supervisors at NT$1.317 million, followed by professionals at NT$1.062 million. These are overall averages — in other words, if you're after the top tier of an industry, your number has to be clearly above this level to be convincing.

One caveat: not every search is solved by paying more. Senior candidates often change jobs for reasons other than money — more decision-making authority, a clearer succession path, less internal friction all have real trade value. What matters is knowing what you're competing with. If the pay is only at market level and the stage isn't any bigger, you're really just relying on luck.

Bottleneck 3: Decision-makers join the process too late

Many companies apply the same process as for ordinary roles: HR screening → hiring manager → the level above → the general manager. Going through all of it can take six to eight weeks.

This sequence breaks down in executive hiring, for two reasons. First, senior candidates have limited time to invest, and if the early rounds keep asking the same "what have you done" questions, they often drop out midway. Second, the person who actually makes the decision only appears at the end, so all the earlier evaluation may be wasted. We have seen a candidate go through four rounds, then get rejected after a 40-minute talk with the general manager because of "a mismatch in style." That call could have been made in the first week.

A more effective setup is to bring the final decision-maker in earlier: the consultant first does an in-depth check of background and motivation, then immediately arranges a conversation with the decision-maker, and only after confirming you're aligned do you move on to further evaluation. There may not be fewer rounds, but far less of the process is wasted.

Sticking point 4: interview design isn't adjusted to the level of the role

Recruiting senior talent the same way you recruit for general roles leads to two kinds of mismatch:

StageCommon approach (same as for ordinary roles)The right approach for executive roles
Focus of questionsConfirm skills and tool proficiencyAsk about decisions: what options there were, why they chose as they did, and how it turned out
Information flowOne-way — the company asks, the candidate answersTwo-way—let them ask about the organization's current state and challenges too
Assessment toolRésumé + interviewPlus a scenario presentation or a 100-day plan discussion
ParticipantsHR and the direct managerIncluding future peer department heads — fit is usually decided here
Background checkA formality, often skippedDone thoroughly with consent, focusing on management style and conflict handling

One more caution about case presentations: asking candidates to present is reasonable, but asking them to hand over a plan you can use directly is not. The assignment should be designed to reveal how they think, not to get free consulting in disguise. Word of this travels fast in executive circles, and overdoing it can hurt a company's reputation in the market.

Practical tips

In executive hiring, everything the company does is being evaluated. Slow replies, late interviewers, the same questions in every round, promised timelines that keep slipping: for regular roles you might get away with this, but for senior candidates these are clear negative signals. If decisions are this slow and the team this uncoordinated, it will probably be the same once they join.

We've seen it more than once: a candidate declines at the final stage, and the reason isn't pay or the role but "the whole process made me doubt how this company operates."

Bottleneck 5: confidentiality and internal communication not planned

Executive hiring almost always involves sensitive situations: the incumbent is still in the seat, someone internally is waiting for that promotion, or the role itself reveals strategic direction. Without advance planning, things can easily go wrong midway:

  • Word leaks internally: someone who was waiting for that promotion starts job hunting, the role still isn't filled, and you lose one more person
  • Word gets out to the market: competitors learn you're replacing someone and use the moment to poach your team
  • A candidate's identity gets exposed: this does the most damage. It affects the candidate's current job, the search falls through, and your reputation in the market suffers too

In practice, at the time of engagement you define who is allowed to know about the search (usually no more than three people), how resumes are passed along (not through ordinary email groups), and when reference checks happen (always last, with the candidate's consent, using contacts they choose). None of this is complicated, but it must be agreed at kickoff; fixing it afterward is very hard.

When should you engage a headhunter, and when don't you need one?

Not every opening needs to be outsourced. Our own rule of thumb is roughly this:

ScenarioRecommendation
Public postings bring enough résumés of usable qualityIn-house hiring is enough; no need to outsource
Needs to stay confidential (incumbent still in the role, strategy involved)Suited to an engaged search: public channels won't work
Target candidates are mostly passive and won't apply on their ownSuited to an engaged search: requires proactive outreach
Highly niche fields beyond the reach of internal networksBest for a retained search—what you're paying for is the candidate list and industry knowledge
Have searched on your own for three months or more and interviewed, but no one fitReview the five bottlenecks above first, then decide whether to outsource
A clear gap between the budget and the market rateDeal with the budget first; outsourcing won't change that fact

The last row deserves emphasis. A headhunter can speed up the search and reach people that public channels can't, but cannot close a gap between your pay and the market rate. If a consultant guarantees results before even seeing your budget, that's a reason to be careful. FAQ

After engaging a search for a senior role, how long does it usually take to see results?+

From engagement to start date, three to six months is typical. The first one to two months usually go to market mapping and outreach, the middle to interviews and evaluation, and finally there's the handover period at the candidate's current job (often one to three months for senior roles). If a consultant promises candidates within two weeks, it's worth asking whether that comes from an active search or an existing database list — the latter usually fits less well.

Will engaging several headhunters at once be faster?+

Not necessarily, and for senior roles it often backfires. With several firms working at once, the same candidate may be approached two or three times by different consultants, which makes the company look desperate or poorly managed and actually lowers interest. It also invites disputes over who "owns" a candidate. Running firms in parallel can work for mid-level roles and below, but the more senior the role, the more we recommend a single engagement with information and confidentiality managed in one place.

How much detail do I need to give the consultant?+

The more detail, the better the match. Beyond the JD, we suggest telling us: why the position is open, what happened with the previous person, the direct manager's management style, the team's current state and known problems, the salary band and how much flexibility there is, and what results you expect within a year. This directly affects whether the consultant can make a convincing case when approaching candidates. If sensitive information is involved, we can sign an NDA before you share it.

If an internal candidate is waiting for promotion, do we still need to look outside?+

It depends on your goal. If the internal candidate is effectively already chosen and you just want to “benchmark externally,” we recommend not starting at all: it wastes candidates' and consultants' time, and once word gets out it hurts your reputation. If you genuinely haven't decided, an external assessment makes sense, but plan your internal communication in advance: how you'll talk to the internal candidate if they aren't chosen, and whether there's an alternative development path for them. When this isn't handled, you often gain one person and lose another.

Can you ask for a guarantee period on senior roles?+

Yes, it's standard practice. If the candidate leaves during the guarantee period, the consultant usually provides a free replacement or a pro-rated refund. The exact number of days and conditions depend on the contract, so confirm three things before signing: how the guarantee period is calculated (from the start date), the conditions for replacement and refunds, and which situations aren't covered (for example, the position being eliminated due to a company reorganization).

If the salary band can't change, what other leverage do we have?+

Several are often underestimated: decision authority (can they build their own team, what's their budget approval limit), who they report to directly (one fewer reporting layer is often more attractive than a 10% raise), a clear succession or promotion path, flexible work arrangements, and how the performance bonus cap is designed. For senior candidates these often matter more than base salary, and they have a smaller impact on your fixed costs. The key is to decide before you approach anyone what you can offer and how far you can go.
