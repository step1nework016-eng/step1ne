Finding a tech or semiconductor headhunting firm: how to assess industry-specialist headhunters and what to watch for when working together

By Step1ne Executive Search Team Published 2026-10-19 About 7 min
Short answer

Tech and semiconductor roles call for industry-specialist headhunters, because almost everyone in this field is currently employed, job functions are finely divided, and pay structures are complex; generalist headhunters often can't even pin down what the role is about. To judge whether a headhunter knows the industry, ask three things: can they explain the differences between job functions, do they know which companies the talent is at, and do they understand how to compare total compensation. Before working together, also confirm whether the fee is calculated only on base salary, and whether the candidate has a non-compete agreement.

1Why do tech and semiconductor companies especially need headhunters?

Finding people in tech and semiconductors comes with a few difficulties rarely seen in other industries:

  • Good people are almost all employed: experienced engineers mostly stay put at major companies or in the supply chain and don't post resumes on job sites, so you have to go and find them.
  • Functions are highly specialized: they're all called "engineers," but process, equipment, integration, facilities, and QA need completely different people — search in the wrong direction and the effort is wasted.
  • Small circles, fast-moving news: advertising publicly can tip off competitors about your plans, and poaching can easily sour relationships, so many companies prefer to proceed confidentially.
  • Complex compensation: beyond monthly salary there's the year-end bonus, profit sharing, employee compensation and stock. Candidates compare total compensation, so offering a monthly salary alone rarely closes the deal.
  • Special working conditions: shift work, cleanrooms, factory-site locations, or postings overseas or in other cities all affect candidates' interest, so make them clear up front.

3Industry-specialist vs. generalist headhunters: the difference and how to tell

Generalist headhunters take any industry, and their strength is breadth; industry-specialist headhunters work in only a few industries, and their strength is that they understand the need, know where the people are, and can persuade candidates. In your first meeting, you can ask these three questions to judge:

  1. How is this role different from similar ones? For example, ask them to explain how the person they'd look for as a process engineer differs from an integration engineer. If they can answer, their search won't go off course.
  2. Which companies and job levels is this kind of talent mostly in right now? A consultant who knows the industry can describe where the talent is and how job titles and levels map across companies.
  3. How is a candidate's total compensation usually structured? A knowledgeable consultant will bring up year-end bonuses, profit sharing, stock, and shift allowances on their own, and help you judge whether the offer is competitive.

4Salary and fees: agree first on what “salary” includes

Tech candidates compare total compensation, which commonly includes monthly salary, year-end bonus, profit sharing or employee compensation, stock, and shift or on-site allowances. If you only look at monthly salary when making an offer, you can easily lose at the final stage to the current employer's retention offer.

This also affects headhunting fees. Fees are usually calculated on the candidate's salary, so whether "salary" means base pay only or also includes bonuses and stock can make a big difference to the amount. Before engaging a firm, make sure the calculation basis is written into the contract. For fee structures and the variables a quote won't show you, see How headhunting fees are calculated.

5Before poaching: non-compete agreements and trade secrets

The most common problem when poaching in tech is that the candidate has signed a post-employment non-compete agreement with their current employer. Under Article 9-1 of Taiwan's Labor Standards Act, such an agreement is valid only if it meets all four conditions: the employer has a legitimate business interest worth protecting, the worker's position involves access to trade secrets, the period and scope are reasonable, and the employer provides reasonable compensation; moreover, the period may not exceed two years.

What companies can do:

  • In interviews, ask candidates whether they've signed a non-compete agreement, and its duration, scope and compensation terms.
  • For positions with any doubts, have legal counsel or a lawyer assess them before hiring. Don't rely only on what the candidate tells you.
  • Make it clear to candidates that they must not take any materials from their previous employer, so the company isn't drawn into a trade secrets dispute.

6Three tips for working with tech-industry headhunters

  1. Have the hiring manager join the needs interview directly: HR can't always explain the technical details. Ten minutes from the manager can save several rounds of referrals.
  2. Respond quickly to recommendations: strong tech candidates usually have several opportunities at once. Reply a week late, and they may have gone elsewhere.
  3. Lay out the working conditions upfront: shifts, plant location, on-site placement, overseas assignment — the earlier the consultant knows, the sooner unsuitable candidates can be filtered out.

7FAQ

Do semiconductor headhunters charge the same as general headhunters?+

The calculation is broadly the same: usually based on the candidate's salary and charged after they start. The difference is that tech compensation often includes profit sharing and stock, so the contract should first spell out whether the basis is fixed pay only or includes variable pay.

Can we hire a candidate who has a non-compete clause?+

It depends on whether the agreement meets the four conditions of Article 9-1 of Taiwan's Labor Standards Act, with a maximum term of two years. Agreements that don't meet them are invalid, but for an actual determination we recommend having legal counsel or a lawyer assess the specific case.

Can a small company find semiconductor talent?+

Yes, but you'll usually need appeal beyond pay—a broader scope of responsibility, faster promotion, or new technical challenges. An industry-specialist headhunter can help you identify which candidates care most about these things.

For tech roles, should I use a headhunter or recruitment outsourcing?+

Senior, scarce or confidential positions suit headhunting. For high-volume positions with clear requirements (such as technicians or assistant engineers), recruitment outsourcing is usually enough. To see how recruitment outsourcing works, read What is recruitment outsourcing?.
