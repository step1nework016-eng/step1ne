How are headhunting fees calculated? Four things to negotiate beyond the rate

By: Step1ne Executive Search Consultants Published 2026-07-27 About 5 min
Short answer

Most headhunting in Taiwan is "contingency" (paid on success), with fees calculated as a percentage of the hire's annual salary. The common market range is about 20%–30%, and the more senior and scarce the role, the higher the percentage. But what really determines the final amount isn't that percentage. It's four variables you won't see on the quote: how annual salary is defined, whether there is a minimum fee, the guarantee period and replacement terms, and how candidate ownership is determined. Even at the same 25%, different terms on these four points can change the total fee by over a hundred thousand NT dollars.

Typical market rates are around 20%–30% of annual salary, and the more senior and scarce the role, the higher the percentage tends to be. Actual figures vary by industry, difficulty, and depth of the relationship.

Four things not shown on the quote that still affect the total

Two headhunters both quoted "25% of annual salary," yet the final bills differed by over NT$100,000. The difference usually comes down to these four items:

1. How annual salary is defined

This is the item with the biggest gap. Does "annual salary" mean only 12 months of base pay, or does it include year-end bonus, performance bonus, signing bonus, and stock? Take a monthly salary of NT$80,000 with a 2-month year-end bonus: calculated on 12 months it's NT$960,000; on 14 months it's NT$1,120,000. At the same 25%, the fee differs by NT$40,000. Be sure to put the calculation basis in writing before signing.

2. Whether there's a minimum fee

Some headhunters set a minimum fee, for example “25%, but no less than NT$300,000.” When hiring for entry- or mid-level roles, the minimum can end up higher than the percentage-based amount. If you're filling a mid-level role, be sure to ask about this.

3. Guarantee period and replacement terms

Guarantee periods typically run 30–120 days. But the number of days isn't the only thing to pin down: Is it a free replacement or a refund? What percentage is refunded? How many replacements are allowed? Does it apply if the company itself lays the person off? A contract that only says "90-day guarantee" easily leads to conflicting interpretations when something goes wrong.

4. Determining who a candidate "belongs" to

If a candidate the headhunter recommends is someone you've contacted before or already have in your database, how is the fee handled? The sensible approach is to agree on a rule for “duplicate candidates,” for example no fee if you show a prior contact record within a set number of business days after receiving the recommendation. Without such an agreement, this is the item most likely to cause disputes.

A headhunter's view

Almost every company negotiating fees tries to cut the percentage, but the percentage is usually the least flexible item. It is the market rate; push it too low and the headhunter won't invest resources, and you'll end up with candidates other clients passed on.

What's actually easy to negotiate, and more valuable to you, are three other things: a longer guarantee period, installment payments tied to the hire staying on, and a committed pace of candidate submissions. For example, extending the guarantee from 90 to 120 days doesn't cost the headhunter much, but gives you much more real protection. Spending your negotiating energy here pays off more than shaving off 3%.

How do you tell whether a quote is reasonable?

Don't just compare fee percentages; work backward from these three angles:

  • Convert it into "total cost per hire": add up the service fee, internal interview time, and the operating losses while the position sits empty. The cost of leaving a key role vacant for three months often far exceeds those few percentage points of difference in rates
  • Judge by quality of submissions, not quantity: sending 20 résumés for you to sift through versus sending 5 that are all worth interviewing; the latter saves your managers' time
  • Ask clearly "who will actually do the work": at the same rate, a senior consultant handling it personally and handing it to a newcomer produce very different results

When shouldn't you use a headhunter?

To be honest, not every opening is worth this fee. In the following cases, recruiting on your own is usually more cost-effective:

  • Posting the role publicly brings in enough suitable applicants
  • The role is highly replaceable and market supply is ample
  • The salary band is clearly below market (a headhunter can't fix the root problem; you'd just be paying to confirm it)

Headhunting really pays off when the role is confidential and can't be advertised, needs a rare combination of skills, must be filled in large numbers quickly, or has failed repeatedly through in-house hiring. In these cases, what you're buying is the ability to "reach people who aren't looking at job ads."

FAQ

Can you negotiate headhunter fees?+

The percentage itself has limited flexibility because it reflects market rates; push it too low and the headhunter puts in fewer resources, which ultimately hurts the quality of candidates. It's more effective to adjust other terms: extend the guarantee period, split payment into installments after the start date, and agree on a clear schedule and number of submissions. These often protect you far more than the few percentage points you'd save.

Do we pay if no one is found?+

Under a contingency model, you generally pay nothing if no one is placed. Under a retained model, the upfront payment at signing is usually non-refundable even if the search doesn't close, since it pays for the resources the headhunter commits. Always confirm which model applies before signing.

Why do headhunters' quotes vary so much?+

It mostly comes down to three variables: the calculation basis (whether annual salary includes bonuses), whether there is a minimum fee, and the depth of service (whether it includes background checks, offer negotiation, and post-hire follow-up). When comparing prices, ask each firm to calculate the total under "the same assumptions" to see the real difference.

Is high-volume hiring also charged as a % of annual salary?+

Usually not. For projects hiring ten or more people at once, a percentage of annual salary would be too expensive for the employer, so the market typically switches to project-based pricing, for example 1.5–2 months' salary per hire, invoiced in installments as each batch starts. This model is fairer for both sides.

What if the candidate leaves after we've paid?+

Check the guarantee clause. A common arrangement is one free replacement, or a partial refund, if the hire leaves during the guarantee period. The key is to confirm in advance: how many replacements, how many days, and which situations aren't covered (e.g., reorganization or layoffs on the company's side).
