How to choose a headhunting firm in Taiwan: 7 criteria you can actually verify

By the Step1ne executive search consulting team Published 2026-07-27 About 7 min read
Short answer

Don't choose a headhunting firm by size or reputation; look at seven things you can verify: whether it holds a valid employment services license, whether it knows your industry, whether it can explain concretely "how it finds people," whether fees and guarantees are written into the contract, whether the same consultant stays with you from start to finish, the quality of its submissions, and whether it is willing to tell you the truth before taking the job. You can get answers to all seven in the first meeting; you don't have to wait until after you've signed.

Many people search for "recommended headhunting firms in Taiwan" hoping someone will just hand them a list. But the quality of headhunting depends heavily on "the consultant actually handling your search," not the company name, so a list doesn't really help. What is more useful is a set of criteria you can verify yourself, which work no matter which headhunter you are dealing with.

Let's be honest up front

This article was written by a headhunting firm, so of course you might suspect we're promoting ourselves. That's why we don't list any "recommended firms" here; we only give you verification methods you can use with any headhunter (including us). Ask through these seven points and you'll be able to tell the good from the bad yourself.

What is a headhunting firm? Five common types in Taiwan

An executive search firm (often called a headhunting firm) is a licensed employment service company that is commissioned by employers to proactively find people for specific roles. Unlike job sites, which wait for applications, headhunters reach out directly to people who fit but aren't job hunting yet, and most charge only after the candidate starts. The people who actually do the search are the firm's consultants; to see how they work, read How headhunting consultants find people.

Headhunting firms in Taiwan fall roughly into five types. We won't list names, but knowing the types first makes it easier to decide which kind to approach:

TypeFeaturesBetter suited for
International headhuntersHeadquartered overseas, with many international officesForeign companies, multinational roles, and jobs that require English communication
Local Taiwanese full-serviceFamiliar with Taiwanese corporate culture, covering a wide range of rolesMid- to senior-level roles of all kinds at mid-size and large Taiwanese companies
Industry specialistFocus on just one or two industries, such as semiconductors, healthcare, or financeRoles with high technical barriers, a small talent pool, and jargon you need to understand
Platform-affiliatedHeadhunting services offered separately by job banks or job platformsCompanies already using that platform that want to extend into proactive sourcing
Solo practiceSenior consultants working independently, small scaleRoles where having the same consultant involved throughout matters

1Do they hold a valid employment services license?

This is the only item with a legal threshold, and the one most people skip. Under Taiwan's Employment Service Act, a private employment service agency must apply to the competent authority for a license and obtain it before operating, and the license must be renewed periodically.

Verifying is simple: ask them directly for their unified business number and their employment service license number (in a format like "北市就服字第 XXXX 號"). Then check the business number against the business registration lookup system of Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs to confirm the company is operating normally. A legitimate company will give you this right away, and may even list it on its website. If they hedge, be careful.

You can simply ask: "Could you share your company's business registration number and employment services license number? Our internal process requires us to keep them on file."

2Do they know your industry and function?

"We can take any role" usually means they specialize in nothing. A headhunter's core value is knowing the people in the field, knowing which companies the strong performers are at, and understanding the technical details candidates talk about. There's no shortcut to this—it only comes from having done it.

The test isn't asking “Have you worked in this industry?”—no one will say no. Ask for details instead: have them describe the current market for this role. Someone who really knows will tell you which kinds of companies the talent is concentrated in, roughly what the pay band is, and which experience level is hardest to find right now; someone who doesn't will just say “our database is huge.”

You can ask directly: “In your view, what's the current market salary range for this position? What backgrounds do suitable candidates usually come from?”

To use ourselves as an example, our observations on the talent market for construction engineering, BIM, and semiconductor plant construction are collected in our Construction Engineering Jobs & Talent column — you can use it to judge how well we know this field.

3Can they explain how they'll find people?

This question best separates a headhunter from a “résumé forwarder.” Some vendors, once they receive your job description, just repost it on a job bank, wait for applications, and pass the résumés on to you—in which case you could post it yourself and skip the headhunting fee.

A real headhunter should be able to explain their proactive outreach methods specifically: the size and update process of their talent database, how they target passive candidates who are currently employed, which channels they use to reach them, and roughly how soon they can deliver the first shortlist. The answers should be concrete enough that you can tell they have a method, not just adjectives.

Ask them directly: "If you started today, which channels would you use to find people? Roughly when would the first candidates reach me?"

4Are fees and guarantee terms clearly stated in writing?

Most headhunting in Taiwan works on a "success fee" basis, with the fee calculated as a percentage of the hire's annual salary. But the devil is in the details — make sure these items are spelled out in the contract:

ItemWhat to confirm
Calculation basisIs annual salary base pay only, or does it include year-end bonuses, other bonuses and stock? Different bases can change the total fee by tens of percent
When fees are chargedPaid in full at start date, or in installments after the hire starts? Is there an upfront retainer (retained model)?
Guarantee periodWithin how long after a departure is a free replacement provided? Commonly 30–120 days. How many replacements? Or is it a refund instead, and how much?
Duplicate candidatesIf your company has also been in touch with that candidate, how is ownership decided? This is the most frequently disputed point

5Does one consultant stay with you start to finish?

Some firms work as "sales closes the deal, an assistant does the work": you talk to a senior consultant, but the person actually doing the work is new to the industry. That isn't necessarily bad, but you should know who is actually responsible, how experienced they are, and whom to contact if something goes wrong.

Ask directly: "Will you personally be the one searching for and contacting candidates? If not, could I meet the colleague who will be?"

6You can judge submission quality from the first batch

The real acceptance test after you start working together is the first batch of candidates. Good submissions come with the consultant's assessment: why they recommend this person, where they fit, where the gaps are, and their current motivation and expected pay. Poor ones just forward the résumé as is.

If the first batch of candidates clearly doesn't fit the spec, there's no need to give up right away—but give feedback immediately and watch how fast they adjust. A headhunter who asks you “why wasn't this a fit?” is far more useful than one who silently sends ten more résumés.

Do they run a structured initial screening before recommending candidates?

It should. A good headhunter will have a full conversation with the candidate against your requirements, then have a consultant review it, before sending anyone to you, rather than forwarding résumés that look similar. You can tell whether they've done this from three things:

  • Initial screening should ask about your qualifications, not generic questions. For example, if the role requires Japanese, the screening should actually include a conversation in Japanese to confirm it, not just look at certificates.
  • Salary, start date, and work location are confirmed before submission. These three are what most often derail things after interviews; the earlier they're confirmed, the less of your interview time is wasted.
  • Every candidate comes with an easy-to-read assessment report. It spells out which criteria they meet and what you still need to confirm in the interview.

Take our own process as an example: before a candidate is recommended, AI first conducts an initial interview based on the job requirements, then a consultant reviews the report before it reaches the company. The AI's job is to ask every candidate the same set of questions; judgment and recommendations remain the consultant's responsibility.

Just ask directly: "Before you send a candidate, what do you discuss with them? Can I see what an actual assessment report looks like?"

7Will they tell you the truth before taking the search?

This one is the softest, but often the most accurate.

If your job requirements are clearly unrealistic for the market — pay below the going rate, a skill combination that barely exists, a remote location with no support — a responsible headhunter will tell you before taking the job and suggest adjustments. Those who just want to land the assignment and deal with "can't find anyone" later usually end up wasting both sides' time.

A headhunter's view

When we take on a search ourselves, we always tell the client honestly at the first meeting what makes the role hard. One company was stunned on the spot—because every firm they'd talked to before had said, "No problem, leave it to us."

But the value of honesty shows up later: if you know from the start whether the role is stuck on salary band or on location, you have a chance to adjust before investing three months. Firms that won't tell you the truth before taking the job usually save the hard news for when you no longer have a way out.

Three common pitfalls

  1. Engaging five firms at once in a pitch-style contest looks like covering all the bases, but in reality each firm knows it has only a 20% chance, so none of them invests resources, and in the end all five wait for someone else to move. Either work seriously with a small number of firms, or grant exclusivity tied to a clear timeline and exit terms
  2. Comparing fee rates but not quality: If the rate differs by 3% but the quality of candidates is twice as good, what you pay for is interview time and the cost of an empty seat — far more expensive than 3%
  3. Not spelling out who can and can't be approached. If there are companies you don't want approached (partners, companies in the same group), list them in writing as off-limits when you engage. Finding out afterward that someone was poached from your own partner is very hard to handle

What are the downsides of using a headhunting firm?

Using a headhunting firm isn't without costs. Knowing these downsides before you commission one makes it easier to judge whether it's worth it, and how to reduce the risks:

DrawbacksWhat actually happensHow to reduce it
Costs more than posting ads yourselfUsually calculated from the candidate's salary; the fee for one hire is typically higher than several months of job postingsOutsource only hard-to-fill, rare-skill or confidential roles; hire for ordinary roles yourself
Takes time to communicate your needsIf the requirements aren't clear, the consultant searches in the wrong direction, and going back and forth wastes timeHave the hiring manager join the first requirements interview directly
Consultant quality varies widelyWithin the same firm, results can vary a lot between senior and junior consultantsAsk who will handle your search, and evaluate that consultant against the seven criteria in this article
Confidentiality isn't always well handledAn unprofessional consultant may describe a confidential role in too much detail, and word gets out to competitorsThe contract defines the scope of confidentiality, and any external description gets your approval first
Disputes over candidate ownership are commonWhen you engage several firms at once, the same candidate may be recommended by two of themAgree in the contract that whoever referred first, with a written record, gets credit
Not suited to large numbers of frontline rolesFor high-volume roles like retail staff, customer service, and operators, headhunting tends to cost too muchTo switch to job boards, recruitment outsourcing or staffing, see How to choose recruitment outsourcing for entry- and mid-level roles

If your role isn't hard to fill, your budget is limited, or your needs aren't clear yet, it's usually more cost-effective not to use a headhunting firm for now.

FAQ

Which headhunting firms are in Taiwan? Why doesn't this article list them?+

Headhunting firms in Taiwan fall into roughly five types: international, local generalist, industry specialist, platform-attached, and solo practices. Since we're a headhunting firm ourselves, any list we made couldn't be fully neutral—and the quality of a partnership mainly depends on the consultant in charge—so this article offers only a way to judge. Decide which type to approach based on the role, then compare firms one by one using the seven criteria here.

Is a big headhunting firm always better?+

Not necessarily. Quality in headhunting depends heavily on “the consultant who actually handles your search,” not the company's brand. Large firms' strengths are database scale and cross-regional resources; small or boutique firms' strengths are consultant seniority and level of commitment. The practical approach is to evaluate the person who will handle your search directly, rather than the company.

Do headhunters charge job seekers?+

Legitimate headhunting services charge the employer, not the job seeker. Under Taiwan's relevant regulations, private employment services agencies may not withhold job seekers' property or collect placement deposits, nor charge any fees beyond the prescribed standards. If a provider asks a job seeker to pay before arranging an interview, be on guard — you can check with your local labor authority.

When should you use a headhunter, and when is hiring yourself enough?+

If posting a job publicly gets you candidates, hiring in-house is the most cost-effective. Headhunting truly adds value when the role is confidential and can't be advertised, requires rare specialized skills, needs a large number of people filled in a short time, or you've tried recruiting yourself several times without success — because these situations require proactively reaching passive candidates who "aren't looking at job ads."

Can we start with a small trial?+

Yes, and we recommend it. Especially for mass hiring, start with a first batch of candidates as a market test that checks three things at once: whether the JD matches what the market can actually supply, whether your interview and offer pace keeps people engaged, and whether the salary range is attractive enough. Scaling up after the pilot works is far safer than going all in from the start.
