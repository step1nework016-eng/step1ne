Can I switch careers to BIM engineering without a related degree?

By the Step1ne executive search consulting team Published 2026-07-27 About 7 min read
Short answer

Yes. BIM currently has a large talent gap, and many companies have relaxed requirements to accept any major and provide in-house training, with starting pay of about NT$35,000–45,000. The real barrier isn't your major but three things: whether you can use Revit, whether you can read drawings, and whether you're willing to work at the pace of a construction site or fab. The most common reason non-majors get screened out isn't their education either. It's that their résumé has nothing proving "I can actually build a model."

Why does BIM accept people from unrelated majors?

(Still not sure what BIM engineers actually do? Start with What is a BIM engineer, and how is it different from a drafter?)

It's not charity; it's supply and demand. BIM (Building Information Modeling) has only been adopted at scale in Taiwan in the past few years. Public works and high-tech fab projects have increasingly required BIM deliverables, but university training hasn't kept pace, creating a gap where demand arrived first and talent is still catching up.

As a result, rather than waiting for graduates in the field, companies train people themselves. Especially for large fab construction projects that need to bring in many people at once, they almost always post roles with "any major, training provided," because in the labor market you simply cannot find enough candidates who have both the right degree and experience.

A headhunter's view

The hiring managers we work with are usually far more open to candidates from other majors than job seekers expect. What you'll really be asked isn't "Why didn't you study civil engineering?" but "Why do you want to do this, and how long do you plan to stay?"—because training a newcomer is expensive, and what managers fear most is teaching someone everything only to see them leave.

In other words, the disadvantage of coming from an unrelated field is at the resume stage (no keywords for HR to spot at a glance); once you reach the interview, it's something you can turn around. The hard part is getting to the interview.

Three things employers actually look at

1. Can you actually produce work in Revit?

This is the one hard requirement with no room for negotiation. The core of BIM work is turning designs into models with software such as Revit, so if you can't use it, you can't do the job. But the required level is lower than you might think: entry-level roles need you to "build components according to the standards," not "master every advanced feature."

2. Can you read drawings?

Models are built from design drawings. If you can't read floor plans, sections, and MEP system drawings, you can't turn 2D drawings into a 3D model. Graduates in the field do have an advantage here, but it's also the easiest gap to close quickly — most BIM courses on the market teach it alongside the software.

3. Willingness to work on site

BIM isn't a pure desk job. Fab projects usually require you to be physically on site, complete safety training, and follow on-site work rules, and overtime is normal during drawing crunches. This isn't about ability but willingness, yet hiring managers care a lot about it, because many people quit after discovering they can't handle it.

A headhunter's view

The most common mistake career changers from other fields make is spending their time on certificates instead of building a portfolio. When we submit candidates, hiring managers almost never grant an interview because of a certificate alone; what they ask is "what models have you built, how big were they, and how did you solve problems along the way?"

Rather than signing up for another course, turn the project model you built in class into a three-to-five-page portfolio write-up: what the original drawings looked like, what you built, where you got stuck, and how you solved it. This does far more than a certificate, and it also solves the résumé-screening problem of “no keywords because I'm from another major.”

If you qualify for government subsidies and have the time, pursuing both paths at once is the most practical approach: take courses to build your software skills while watching for jobs with training programs, and go straight in if you're hired.

Age 29 and under: you can use government subsidies first

Taiwan's Ministry of Labor runs the Industry New Elite Program (產業新尖兵計畫), which subsidizes training costs of up to NT$100,000 for unemployed (or job-seeking) young people aged 15–29 (you pay NT$10,000 upfront and can apply for a refund if you find a job within 90 days of completing the course). Trainees who meet the attendance requirement also receive a learning allowance of NT$8,000 per month for up to 12 months, and the designated courses include BIM/Revit classes. For the current course list and eligibility, please refer to the announcements on the Industry New Elite Program official website.

The three most common reasons career-changer résumés get rejected

  1. No verifiable work samples: just "proficient in Revit," with no model or project to back it up. HR can't judge your level, and the easiest thing for them to do is skip you
  2. Not explaining why you're switching: hiring managers worry about a "just trying it out" mindset. A clear, specific reason for changing careers does more than taking one more course
  3. Not addressing the "on-site" concern: if all your past jobs were in an office, managers will worry you won't cope with a plant or construction site. Explain proactively on your résumé or in the interview that you understand this and can adapt, and the concern goes away

FAQ

With no engineering background at all, can I really do this?+

Yes, you can. Entry-level BIM modeling is essentially "building components according to the drawings"; what it takes is attention to detail and spatial sense, not structural calculation. The stages that really require engineering judgment are the more senior coordination and integration work, and by then you'll have built up hands-on experience on the job. Career changers from design, drafting or drawing backgrounds usually pick it up fastest, but people from unrelated fields have a chance too if they're willing to learn.

Will my starting salary be very low if I switch to BIM?+

Entry-level starting pay is about NT$35,000–45,000 a month, and some openings also offer a guaranteed year-end bonus (commonly 1–2 months), so actual annual income is higher than the monthly figure suggests. This starting point is above average among no-experience jobs, and BIM pay growth comes mainly from moving up job levels rather than piling up years, so after 2–4 years the range widens noticeably.

Can I still switch if I'm older?+

Yes. Taiwan's Employment Service Act prohibits age limits in job ads, and in practice employers assess willingness to learn and stability. If you have experience in manufacturing, construction-related work, or jobs that involve reading drawings, that's actually a plus—people who understand how a site runs have an edge over those with a purely technical background when coordinating across departments.

Should I teach myself first and then apply, or apply right away?+

We recommend doing both at once. Openings that offer "full training, any major" are limited and get snapped up quickly, so they may be closed by the time you finish a course. But applying without ever having touched the software also means a low pass rate at the résumé stage. A workable approach: spend a few weeks going through Revit basics and build one or two models you can talk about, while starting to apply at the same time — filling gaps as you go.
