Operations Engineer Careers: How Do They Compare with Development?

By: Step1ne Executive Search Consultants Published 2026-07-27 About 5 min
Short answer

Operations is not a second-class alternative to development, but the career ceiling really is very different. The key is whether you are "the person who handles incidents" or "the person who designs systems so incidents don't happen." Operations roles that only respond to alerts tend to sit in a lower pay band; people with automation, cloud architecture, and reliability engineering skills are worth as much as senior developers on the market, or more. When you see the title "operations engineer," check whether the JD mentions automation, CI/CD, or IaC to tell which kind it is.

Operations is not "the job for people who couldn't make it in development"

Let's clear up a misconception first. Taiwan's tech community has long seen operations as second-tier to development, but that image comes from one specific kind of ops work: restarting machines, watching monitoring dashboards, and handling alerts by the SOP.

What really sets the ceiling of an operations career is whether you are "handling incidents" or "designing systems so incidents don't happen". The former does tend to keep you in a lower pay band; the latter (automation, observability, architecture improvements) is worth as much on the market as senior development work, sometimes more, because fewer people can do it.

A headhunter's view

The most important question to ask when applying for an operations role is: "How much of this job is spent handling alerts, and how much on improvements?"

If the answer is "mostly handling alerts," it's a burn-out seat—you'll be busy, but two years later you'll have nothing to put on your résumé. If, on the other hand, the team lets you spend time automating repetitive work, those automation results become your bargaining chips for your next job.

We've seen many operations engineers stuck in the same salary band for years, and the reason is almost always the same: they're too busy to do the work that would make them less busy.

How should you evaluate on-call?

Operations roles often come with on-call duty. It's key to your quality of life, yet many people are afraid to ask about it in detail during interviews. Make sure you clarify these four things:

  1. Rotation frequency — how often, and for how long each time? "One week on, once every six weeks" and "once every two weeks" are completely different lives
  2. How often you actually get called: being on standby on paper and actually being woken up every night are two different things. Ask directly: "Roughly how many times were people paged last month?"
  3. Compensation — is there an on-call allowance? How is time spent responding counted as overtime? Can you take time off in lieu the next day?
  4. Escalation path — if you hit a problem in the middle of the night that you can't solve, is there someone to call? On-call without backup is far more stressful

On-call isn't bad in itself; having on-call usually means the system matters, and experience with important systems is valuable. The problem is when the frequency is unreasonable and there is no matching compensation.

How do you move up from operations?

Three paths that actually work:

  • Go deeper into cloud and automation: script repetitive work, learn IaC and container orchestration, and move toward cloud operations or SRE. This is currently the direction with the strongest market demand
  • Moving into development: ops support roles already involve code and CI/CD, so moving into development after building experience isn't unusual—and developers who understand operations are more pragmatic about system design
  • The architecture or management path: ops has seen more ways systems break than anyone, which is a rare instinct when designing architecture

The common key is building a record of "what you improved," not "how many incidents you handled." On a résumé, "Automated the deployment process, cutting release time from 2 hours to 15 minutes" beats "Maintained 200 servers."

What do managers look for when interviewing for operations roles?

Based on real feedback from hiring teams, interviews for operations roles focus on somewhat different things than development roles:

  • Troubleshooting approach—you'll be asked, "How would you investigate if the system slowed down?" The point isn't to give the right answer but to lay out a systematic order of investigation
  • Sensitivity to risk: for example, when asked whether to deploy before the end of the day, the answer itself doesn't matter; what matters is showing that you weigh the risks
  • Communication skills: operations has to coordinate with development, security, PMs and even customers, and people who are technical but can't explain a situation clearly struggle in the role
  • Willingness to learn — cloud and tooling change fast, so managers want to see that you keep your skills up to date

FAQ

Do operations engineers earn less than developers?+

Entry-level operations roles do often pay less than entry-level development, but the gap widens or narrows depending on your skill direction. Operations engineers with automation, cloud architecture, and reliability engineering skills can earn as much as or more than senior developers, because such talent is scarcer in the market. The key isn't the job title but whether you're "the person who handles incidents" or "the person who designs systems so incidents don't happen."

Can I apply for cloud operations without cloud experience?+

For some roles, yes. If you have solid Linux/Windows operations fundamentals, scripting skills, and networking knowledge, quite a few companies are willing to let you learn cloud on the job. In practice, the required qualifications for these roles are often "OS and networking fundamentals," with cloud experience as a plus. Earning an entry-level certification from a cloud vendor first will make your case stronger.

Is on-call as scary as it sounds?+

It depends on how the system is designed. Ask about rotation frequency, how often you actually get paged, how allowances and comp time work, and whether there's an escalation path at night. A reasonable setup (e.g., one-week shifts about once every six weeks, with an on-call allowance) is acceptable to most people; frequent pages with no compensation call for caution.

Can you move from operations into development?+

Yes, and it's not unusual. Operations support roles already involve code, CI/CD, and troubleshooting, so moving into development after building experience is a natural path. The advantage is that developers who understand operations have a real feel for system stability and deployment risk, which makes their designs more practical. We recommend building up a portfolio of code you can show while you're in the operations role.

How do you tell whether an operations role is worth taking?+

Look at two things: first, whether the team sets aside time for improvement (rather than spending all its time firefighting); second, whether it uses a modern toolchain (CI/CD, IaC, observability platforms). These two points determine what you'll be able to put on your résumé two years from now.
