Software engineer salaries: why the same years of experience can differ by 20K across industries

By: Step1ne Executive Search Consultants Published 2026-07-27 About 5 min
Short answer

In Taiwan, front-end engineers average about NT$47,700 a month, back-end about NT$45,000–65,000, and software engineers in general about NT$41,300, while game development roles sit at about NT$32,000–38,000, clearly below general software jobs. The main cause of the gap is not technical difficulty but the industry's ability to pay. Put differently, what drives your salary is "industry > whether you can own a system on your own > tech stack," yet most people focus on the third.

Why are the differences between industries so large?

The same back-end work can pay up to NT$20,000 differently across industries. The reason isn't technical difficulty but whether the industry "can afford" engineers:

  • Finance / e-payments: Regulated, with a high cost of errors and strict system stability requirements — so they're willing to pay more to keep people
  • Semiconductors / tech manufacturing—high margins in the core business, so IT departments have relatively ample budgets
  • E-commerce / SaaS: technology translates directly into revenue, so the return on engineering investment is clear
  • Gaming: Plenty of talent supply (many people want in because they love games), plus high product-cycle risk, keeps overall pay on the low side
A headhunter's view

This is the thing we most often need to be upfront about when talking with engineers: pay in the gaming industry is generally lower than in finance, e-commerce, and semiconductors. That's structural, not individual companies being stingy.

So what you trade for when entering the gaming industry is something else: interest in the product itself, the type of technical challenge (high concurrency, real-time interaction), and team culture. If your top goal is maximizing salary, gaming is usually not the shortest path; but if you care about whether you personally connect with what you build, the trade may be worth it. There's no right answer here, but think it through before you join, rather than discovering the gap two years in.

Does your tech stack affect your pay?

Yes, but not quite in the way most people think.

You often hear online that “.NET has no future; learn Go if you want money.” The reality: your tech stack decides which industries you can enter, and the industry decides the salary band. In Taiwan, .NET demand is steady in finance, manufacturing, and internal enterprise systems, and pay in those industries isn't necessarily low. Go, Kubernetes and similar technologies are concentrated in cloud-native companies and startups, with higher salary bands but fewer openings.

Three things really affect pay, in this order:

  1. Which industry you're in: the biggest factor, with gaps of up to NT$20,000
  2. Can you own a system on your own: going from "writing features to spec" to "designing the architecture and owning performance and stability" is the biggest leap
  3. The tech stack itself — it matters, but only third, and you can get around it by switching industries

How does experience translate into pay?

Salary growth in software roles isn't linear. The key lies in a few thresholds:

StageMarket expectationsThe key to moving up
0–2 yearsCan build features to spec and write maintainable codeMove past the stage of "needing someone to review before daring to ship"
2–5 yearsCan own a module independently and handle production issuesCan pinpoint performance bottlenecks and production issues on their own, not just fix things by following the SOP
5+ yearsCan design architecture, evaluate technology choices, and lead peopleYou start being paid for decisions, not hours
A headhunter's view

In interviews, what makes the biggest difference to pay isn't how many languages you speak but whether you can explain why you do things the way you do.

Among candidates we submitted who were turned down, the most common feedback is "technically capable but can't explain the trade-offs" — for example, when asked why they chose a particular database or designed an API a certain way, the answer is "that's just how the company did it." Conversely, candidates who can explain which options they considered at the time and why they dropped them often get better offers, even with somewhat fewer years of experience.

FAQ

Do game industry engineers really earn less?+

Looking at overall market statistics, yes. Average monthly pay for game development roles is around NT$32,000–38,000, below the NT$45,000–65,000 range for backend engineers. Reasons include an ample talent supply (many people join out of passion) and product-cycle risk. But individual companies vary a lot, and mature titles in stable operation usually pay better than startup teams.

Do .NET engineers still have a future in Taiwan?+

Yes, but the direction matters. .NET has steady demand in finance, manufacturing, and enterprise internal systems, and pay in these industries isn't necessarily low. The real risk isn't the language itself but spending years on repetitive maintenance without building architecture or system design skills; that's what keeps you from getting a good offer when you change jobs.

Should you switch to finance or semiconductors for the pay?+

It's worth considering, but it depends on what you care about. These industries pay more, but compliance requirements are strict, processes are long, and technology choices tend to be conservative. If you enjoy fast iteration and technical freedom, you may not adapt well. Rather than just comparing offer numbers, ask specifically in the interview how development processes and technical decisions work.

How much can you negotiate on salary when changing jobs?+

A reasonable raise when changing jobs is typically about 10%–20%, and it can be higher if it comes with a promotion or an industry switch (for example, from gaming to finance). In practice, the most effective approach is to learn the role's budget range before naming your number; this is one of the real benefits of applying through a headhunter, since the consultant knows the range the company offers.

Do operations roles pay less than development?+

Not necessarily. Entry-level operations roles do often pay less than entry-level development, but operations engineers with automation, cloud architecture, and SRE skills can earn as much as senior developers or more. The difference is whether you're "the person who handles incidents" or "the person who designs systems so incidents don't happen."
