Is there a career in administrative assistance? Five paths that actually work

By: Step1ne Executive Search Consultant Team Published 2026-07-27 About 8 min
Short answer

Yes — but almost always through changing jobs, not internal promotion. That's the single most important reality of administrative careers in Taiwan. It's not that you aren't good enough; most companies simply have small admin teams with no positions above you. In practice, five paths work: HR, project management, purchasing/supply chain, finance and accounting, and executive assistant. The common key isn't collecting more certificates — it's giving the work you're responsible for a name.

First, where the real ceiling is

Many discussions claim "administrative assistants have no future." That's imprecise and not very fair. More accurately: the problem with admin roles isn't a low ceiling, but that the ladder upward often doesn't exist.

What's the difference? If the ceiling were merely low, there'd still be a few rungs to climb. But in reality, Taiwan is dominated by small and medium-sized businesses; a company's admin team may be just two or three people, reporting to an admin manager or directly to the owner. If the person in that seat isn't leaving, you have nowhere to go.

This is a structural problem, not a capability problem. Recognizing that matters, because it decides where you should put your effort: not into "performing a bit better to get promoted," but into building things you can take with you.

A headhunter's view

We've seen a lot of administrative résumés, and the most common problem is that after five years, all they can say is still “assisted manager with daily administrative tasks, document management, and meeting arrangements.”

It's not that these people do a poor job—quite the opposite; many are so good the whole office can't function without them. The problem is that what they do has no name.

"Handling odds and ends" is not a job function, but "vendor management," "travel policy administration," and "new-hire onboarding" are. Do the same work under a name and keep records of it, and in three years your résumé will look completely different. This is the most important, and least discussed, point about an admin career.

Path 1: HR — the smoothest transition

If your work already touches attendance, labor and health insurance, or you've helped post job ads, you're one step away from HR. That step is turning "I've handled it" into "I understand it": why insurance brackets are calculated the way they are, how annual leave is granted, what the termination procedure is.

In practice, the best preparation is to volunteer for recruiting coordination work. Starting with scheduling interviews and contacting candidates gives you experience you can talk about directly when changing jobs.

Path 2: Project management, the easiest path to write up on a résumé

People with long admin careers tend to underestimate their coordination skills. Chasing progress, watching deadlines, getting five departments into the same meeting room at the same time — these are core project management moves to begin with.

What's usually missing is just two things: one complete project you can talk about, and the common language of project management (scope, schedule, risk, stakeholders). For the first, we suggest going after it in your current job: office moves, system rollouts, annual events. These are real projects, and admin staff are often the ones running them anyway.

Path 3: Procurement / supply chain

If you already manage stationery, equipment and vendor contracts, you're effectively doing small-scale procurement. To move into a formal purchasing role, what you need to add is negotiation and cost awareness—going from "find one vendor that works" to "compare three, calculate total cost and negotiate better terms."

This path offers more opportunities at mid-size and large companies, because at small companies purchasing is usually scattered within admin work and has no dedicated headcount.

Path 4: Finance and accounting, the most demanding to get into, but the most stable

If you're handling billing, vouchers, and reconciliations, moving into finance and accounting makes sense. But this path is different: it requires real professional upskilling, usually through accounting courses or relevant certifications — it's not a natural transition based on your current experience alone.

The advantage is that accounting and finance careers have a clear ladder: accounting clerk, senior accountant, accounting manager. Unlike administration, there are positions above you. For those willing to invest the time, it's the most stable of the five paths.

Path 5: Executive assistant, with few openings but the biggest jump

The biggest difference between an executive assistant and an administrative assistant isn't the tasks — it's whether you act as an extension of your manager's thinking. An admin assistant handles assigned tasks; an executive assistant anticipates, pulls together the information needed for decisions, and sometimes speaks on the manager's behalf.

This path has few openings and depends a lot on luck (whose assistant you become, and whether that person is willing to mentor you), but the pay jump is the biggest of the five. To prepare, sharpen your written communication: whether you can turn meeting notes into something with conclusions, action items and owners is a very direct test.

Something to face honestly

Pure paperwork is indeed being replaced by automation. Data entry, form compilation, and scheduling are already handled quite well by tools, and they keep getting better.

That doesn't mean admin jobs will disappear. What will disappear are the positions that "only do these things." The parts that require judgment, coordination, and adaptability, such as two departments fighting over the same resource, on-the-spot decisions in emergencies, and greeting and calming people face to face, can't be replaced any time soon.

The practical move isn't to resist but to flip it around: proactively use these tools to clear repetitive work, then spend the time saved on coordination and process improvement. That in itself is the best preparation for a career move.

Four things you can start in your current job

You don't have to wait until you're changing jobs to prepare. These four things can be done now, and none of them need your manager's approval:

  1. Give your responsibilities a name — don't write "assisted with miscellaneous tasks." If what you manage is "company-wide vendor contact and contract renewal tracking," write it and say it that way
  2. Record what can be measured: "Reworked the expense reimbursement process, cutting average payout time from 10 days to 4," "Introduced a new meeting-room booking method, reducing scheduling conflicts." The numbers don't need to be impressive; they just need to exist
  3. Volunteer for the projects nobody wants: office moves, system rollouts, annual events, audit prep. These are among the few chances admin staff get to build "project experience," and usually no one competes with you for them
  4. Pick one direction and fill in the knowledge for it — you don't need to prepare for all five paths. Choose one and fill the gaps. Preparing all five at once usually leaves none of them deep enough
A headhunter's view

One last piece of advice, about the timing of a career move.

There's a common trap when changing administrative jobs: switching because you're "bored of it," only to end up doing the same thing at another company. We've seen plenty of people go through three companies with job content no different from five years ago — only the office changed.

Before changing jobs, ask yourself one question: after I move, what will my résumé have that it doesn't have now? If you can't answer, maybe what needs to change isn't the company but the scope of your role, and that you can do in your current position.

FAQ

Is it too late to change careers after three years in admin?+

No—three years is actually a relatively good time. You have enough work experience to talk about and haven't been pigeonholed yet. What's harder in practice is passing seven or eight years without your scope ever expanding; then you have to work harder to explain why you can do the new role. If you're already in that range, we suggest first pushing to expand your scope in your current job and building one or two concrete results before starting your move.

Do you need a certificate first to move from admin into HR?+

It isn't required. Entry-level HR jobs (HR assistant, recruiter) usually care more about tasks you've actually handled, such as attendance management, labor and health insurance, and interview scheduling. A certificate gives you something to point to when you have no related experience at all. If you've already handled these tasks in your current job, describing them specifically on your résumé is more convincing than a certificate. What we really recommend investing in is understanding the basic framework of labor law, because it will come up in interviews.

Can I move into PM without having led a project?+

Yes, but first you need to find something in your current job that qualifies as a project. Office moves, system rollouts, annual events, and audit preparation all count; what they have in common is a clear start and end, multiple stakeholders, and a deliverable. When it's done, organize the background, your role, the problems you hit, and the final outcome into a three-minute story. In interviews this works far better than any certificate. If your current job offers no such opportunities at all, consider moving into a project assistant role first as a stepping stone.

Will I have to take a pay cut to switch careers?+

When moving into an entry-level role in a completely new field, pay may stay flat or dip slightly, but the drop is usually small and is typically recovered and exceeded within one to two years. Bigger drops tend to happen with "no common ground" hard switches — for example, going straight from an administrative role to an engineering role. If you move from a direction with natural overlap (HR, purchasing, project work), flat pay or a modest increase is the common outcome. When negotiating, list concretely the related work you already do in your current job; that's the basis for your negotiation.

Will big companies recognize admin experience from a small company?+

Yes — and sometimes it's an advantage. Admin staff at small companies usually juggle many tasks alone, so their exposure is broader than admin roles at large companies with narrow divisions of labor. What matters is how you present it. Large companies value process and structure, so when describing your experience, cover "how you did it," not just "that you did it" — for example, how you set up a particular process or handled exceptions. That convinces them you can adapt to a more structured environment.

What's the worst case if I stay in admin?+

What deserves the most attention isn't stagnant pay but a scope of work that hasn't grown for a long time. That leaves you with fewer choices when you're forced to change jobs (downsizing, reorganization), since you can only apply for jobs similar to what you have now. Avoiding this doesn't necessarily mean quitting. It means making sure that every year or two you take on at least one more responsibility that has a name. If your current company offers no room for that at all, that's the signal to consider moving.
