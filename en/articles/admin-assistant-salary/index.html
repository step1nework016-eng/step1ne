Administrative assistant pay: why can two assistant jobs differ by NT$10,000?

By the Step1ne executive search consulting team Published 2026-07-27 About 7 min read
Short answer

The average monthly salary for administrative assistants in Taiwan is about NT$32,975 (Indeed, updated April 2026), but the actual range runs from 28K to 50K. The gap isn't about "whether you can do admin work" — it comes down to three things: the industry, whether the role has a specialized component, and whether the position is pure support or has its own area of responsibility. Two jobs both called "assistant" can differ by more than NT$10,000 between the IT industry and a traditional trading company.

First see where the overall market sits

Admin salary bands are fairly narrow, but the ceiling varies a lot by job title:

RoleTypical monthly salary rangeThe keys to higher pay
Switchboard / reception28,000–42,000Language skills, company size, and whether admin duties are included
Admin Assistant30,000–40,000Industry, and whether the role includes accounting or HR duties
Sales assistant32,000–45,000Whether it includes order management, quotations or foreign-language clients
Project / product assistant38,000–55,000Industry (tech/finance pay more) and the scope of cross-department coordination
Executive assistant (to senior executives)45,000–80,000Management level, and whether it involves supporting executive decision-making

Ranges reflect common market figures; actual pay varies by industry, region, company size, and individual background. New graduates tend to sit at the lower end of each range, and after 1–3 years of experience there's a chance to move up a level.

Where does the gap come from? Three real variables

1. Industry: the biggest factor

This is the harshest point, and the most overlooked. The same duties — handling documents, arranging meetings, processing expense claims — can come with starting pay that differs by NT$10,000 between semiconductors, fintech and traditional manufacturing.

It's not that your work is harder—it's that the whole company's pay structure is different. High-margin industries have more generous payroll budgets to begin with, and admin roles rise along with them. So if pay matters to you, switching industries usually does more than toughing it out two more years in the same one.

2. Whether the role includes “non-administrative” elements

Purely supportive work, such as answering phones, ordering lunches, handling deliveries and filing, has a clear salary ceiling because it's easy to replace. What really widens the pay gap is when the job mixes in other expertise:

  • Also handling basic bookkeeping (vouchers, payment requests, reconciliation): usually adds 3–5K
  • Also handling HR administration (labor/health insurance changes, attendance management, recruiting contact) — usually 3–6K more
  • Handling order or purchasing processes — usually 3–8K more, with more career options
  • Requires handling real business in a foreign language (not “some knowledge,” but actually dealing with overseas clients)—usually adds 5–10K

3. Are you "supporting others," or do you "own your own area"?

This is rarely explained clearly, but it's what makes the difference three years from now.

If your job is "do whatever the manager hands you," then no matter how many years you put in, what you can write on your résumé stays about the same. If you have a clear area you own — say, vendor management for the whole office, or tracking a department's budget execution — what you accumulate are quantifiable results, and you'll have something to talk about when negotiating pay.

A headhunter's view

When we help companies hire for administrative roles, we ask the hiring manager one question: "If the person in this role left, what would be the biggest headache?"

If the answer is "We'd just bring someone else in and train them for two weeks," it's hard to negotiate a higher salary for that job, because the company itself knows the role is replaceable. If the answer is "He holds all the supplier contacts" or "He knows why each process works the way it does," there's much more room.

For job seekers, this means: proactively making something in your current job "the thing you know best" is the most practical way to prepare for a raise. It's not about hoarding knowledge; it's about taking on the work nobody wants but that would cause trouble if it were missing.

"Hundreds of applicants for a 29K admin assistant job"—what does that mean?

It's true that competition for admin jobs is fierce. A 29K assistant job receiving over a hundred résumés is nothing new in Taiwan. But this needs to be interpreted correctly.

It reflects a low barrier to entry, not that the job has no value. With no required degree or certificate, career changers, people returning to work and new graduates can all apply, so supply is naturally large. But it also means this: if you have even one thing others don't, you're no longer one of those hundred people.

That "something" could be a foreign language, familiarity with a particular system (ERP, sales and inventory), bookkeeping experience, or even just a résumé written more clearly than everyone else's. The flip side of fierce competition is that most competitors haven't actually prepared.

Warning signs

For admin openings that say "negotiable" with vague duties, ask one more question. Salary ranges for admin roles are actually quite transparent, and companies willing to state them clearly usually have more complete HR systems too.

Also, if a job description crams administration, sales, accounting, and HR into one role but pays just over NT$30K, that's not “learning a lot”—that's one person doing three jobs. In the interview, be sure to ask: What share of the work does each part take? How long did the previous person last?

What should you actually confirm when negotiating pay?

Admin roles usually have little room for salary negotiation, but you can widen the gap in total take-home pay through these items:

  1. Whether there's a year-end bonus, and how many months — this is the biggest variable in admin roles. A 33K monthly salary with a 2-month year-end bonus actually comes out higher than 35K with no bonus
  2. How overtime works: admin roles often have hidden overtime like "it gets busy at month-end." Ask whether it's paid as worked, given as time off in lieu, or just "everyone does it"
  3. Whether there are fixed allowances for transport, meals, perfect attendance, etc.: these usually aren't counted in base salary but do get paid out
  4. Whether pay is reduced during probation—if so, confirm the period and the adjusted figure, and have it written into the offer letter
  5. Employment type: permanent, fixed-term contract, or staffing agency? This affects year-end bonuses, raises, and how your seniority accumulates. If it's agency work, read the key legal points of staffing to understand your rights first
A headhunter's view

The most common mistake admin job seekers make: never asking about the pay structure in the interview, for fear of seeming petty.

In reality, asking clearly about year-end bonuses, allowances and overtime rules comes across to hiring managers as "this person is meticulous," not petty — because admin work itself requires that sensitivity to detail. It's the people who accept without asking anything who make managers worry they'll discover a mismatch after joining and leave within three months.

FAQ

Can I apply for an administrative assistant role with no relevant experience?+

Yes. Administrative work is one of the few job categories in Taiwan with relatively low requirements for degree and experience, and it's an entry point for many people changing careers or returning to work. In practice, companies care more about attention to detail, proficiency with office software, and communication attitude. If you have no experience at all, write out concretely any experience that involved "arranging processes, coordinating multiple parties, or handling documents" on your résumé — even student club or part-time work can be convincing.

What's the difference between a receptionist/operator and an admin assistant?+

A receptionist/switchboard role is all about being "the first point of contact with the outside": transferring calls, greeting visitors, handling mail, and managing meeting rooms, with a fixed post and regular hours. An administrative assistant leans toward internal support, with a broader but more scattered scope. Pay is similar for both, but a receptionist at a sizable company who needs to greet visitors in a foreign language may start higher than general admin staff, while an administrative assistant can more easily pick up extra skills like bookkeeping and HR, so there are usually more paths upward.

How often do admin assistant salaries get raised?+

Most companies give raises once a year, often in the 3–5% range. That's the reality for administrative roles: internal raises rarely keep pace with changing jobs. In practice, pay jumps noticeably when you change companies or industries—especially moving from general industries into higher-paying sectors like tech or finance. To win a bigger raise at the same company, you usually need a real expansion of your responsibilities, not just more years of service.

Do certifications help admin salaries?+

The benefit is limited but real — the key is whether the certificate maps to skills you'll actually use. Language tests like TOEIC genuinely help for roles that involve foreign-language communication, and basic accounting certificates add real value when paired with bookkeeping duties. Certificates unrelated to the work, on the other hand, have almost no effect on pay. Rather than taking more tests, it's usually more effective to list the systems you know well (ERP, inventory and sales systems, HR systems, etc.) specifically on your résumé.

Is a one-year contract job worth taking?+

Look at three things: whether the pay is clearly higher than comparable permanent jobs, whether there are real cases of people being made permanent when the term ends, and what you'll gain in that year. If it's a fixed-term role created for a specific project, the pay is usually higher than typical admin jobs and the work tends to be more specialized, which helps your résumé. But ask clearly in advance what happens when the term ends, and confirm the nature of the contract and your labor and health insurance enrollment.

Will AI replace administrative assistants?+

It will replace part of the work — mainly repetitive paperwork, data organizing, and scheduling. But the parts that require judgment, coordination, and thinking on your feet will be hard to replace in the near term: resolving priority conflicts between departments, handling unexpected situations, greeting visitors in person. The practical advice is to actively use these tools to clear away repetitive work and spend the time saved on coordination and process improvement. That's exactly how you move yourself from "replaceable" to "irreplaceable."
